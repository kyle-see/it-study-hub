The essentials — hold these before the detail

The module at entry-certification level: what you must be able to do, and the facts the quizzes and drills on this page are testing. Read this first, every time.

You can — by the end of this module
  • name what the CPU, RAM, storage, PSU and motherboard each do
  • tell RAM from storage from cache, and say what each is for
  • look at a connector or port and say what plugs into it
  • describe the order of an actual build, and why the order matters
  • explain what BIOS/UEFI does, and when the CMOS battery is the fault
  • choose between SATA and NVMe storage and explain the speed gap
Know this — the need-to-know core, 10 facts
CPU
executes instructions. Cores are independent execution units; clock speed in GHz is cycles per second; cache (L1/L2/L3) is the fastest memory and sits on or next to the die.
RAM
volatile working memory. It holds what is running right now and loses everything at power-off. Not enough RAM means the machine swaps to disk.
Storage
persistent. A hard disk spins platters (cheap per gigabyte); an SSD is flash with no moving parts (far faster, more expensive per gigabyte).
Interfaces
SATA tops out far below NVMe, which uses PCIe lanes directly. Same drive size, very different speed.
Motherboard
carries the CPU socket, RAM slots, expansion slots and the chipset. The chipset decides which ports and lanes exist at all.
PSU
converts AC wall power to the DC the parts need. Wattage must cover the load; 80 PLUS ratings describe efficiency, not capacity.
Firmware
BIOS/UEFI runs before any operating system, finds a bootable device and hands over. UEFI replaced BIOS; Secure Boot is a UEFI feature.
Ports
USB-A/USB-C (versions differ in speed, not shape), HDMI and DisplayPort for video and audio, RJ45 for Ethernet, 3.5 mm for audio.
Build order
CPU, thermal paste, cooler, RAM, then the board into the case, then PSU, storage, GPU, cables, POST, then the operating system.
Mobile
the same components soldered and shrunk. Batteries are lithium-ion and wear with heat and charge cycles; repairs are limited by adhesive and part pairing.
The trap: Calling storage 'memory'. Memory is RAM: it is volatile and it is gone at power-off. If it survives a reboot, it is storage.

Everything below this box explains these lines, in this order, with a drill after each part that tests it. If you can say every line above without looking, the drills are revision. If you cannot, read the section that matches the line and come back — that is the whole study method. The same objectives for every module, built and not yet built, are on the learning path.

What is actually inside the box

Five parts do the work. Everything else is cooling, connection, or convenience.

PartJobIf it fails
CPUExecutes instructions: fetch, decode, execute, store — billions of times a second.Nothing boots at all.
RAMHolds what is running right now. Volatile: cleared on power-off.Boots, then crashes or refuses to start programs. Often a beep code.
StorageRemembers when the power is off.Boots to an error, or loses files. The drive is often the thing to suspect first.
MotherboardCarries the buses and slots that connect everything.Dead machine, or one part stops being seen.
PSUConverts wall AC into the DC rails the components need.No power, random shutdowns under load, or nothing at all.
The distinction the whole course turns on: programs are instructions, hardware is the physical stuff that follows them. A program is stored on a drive, copied into RAM to run, and executed by the CPU. Every performance question — "why is it slow?" — is a question about which of those three is the bottleneck.

This module is the parts and how they fit. Module 3 takes the next layer up: the operating system that manages all of it, so that no application has to know which stick of RAM it is using.

Buses, registers and the clock

The parts are useless on their own. These are the wires and the timing that let them talk.

The external data bus (EDB) is a row of parallel wires connecting the parts. Voltage on a wire is a 1; no voltage is a 0. That is how bits physically travel, and it is why the bus has a width — an 8-, 16-, 32- or 64-bit bus moves that many bits at once.

Data bus
Carries the actual values. Wider bus, more bits per transfer.
Address bus
Carries where to read or write. Its width caps how much memory the CPU can address — which is exactly what "32-bit" and "64-bit" mean for memory.
Control bus
Carries the command: read, write, interrupt. The traffic signals, not the traffic.
Registers
A handful of tiny storage slots inside the CPU. To add two numbers, one goes in register A, one in B, the result lands in C. Faster than anything else in the machine, and there are very few of them.
Clock
A wire pulsing at a fixed rate that keeps every operation in step. One pulse is a clock cycle; 3.4 GHz is 3.4 billion cycles per second.

What the memory controller does

The CPU does not reach into RAM directly. It asks the memory controller for a specific address, the controller finds it in RAM and returns it over the bus. In modern chips that controller lives on the CPU die itself, which is why the old "northbridge" chip is gone from your motherboard.

Cache: the reason memory is not good enough

Cache is small, fast memory between the CPU and RAM, holding recently used and frequently used data. It exists because RAM is far slower than the CPU: without it, the processor spends most of its life waiting. The hierarchy is registers → L1 → L2 → L3 → RAM → storage, each level bigger and slower than the one before.

Overclocking means running the CPU above its rated clock. It works, it is measurable, and it costs you: more heat, more power, possible instability, and usually the warranty. Underclocking is the same dial turned the other way, and is what laptops do on battery.

The CPU: package, socket, cores, cooler

The chip that decides. Its performance is a product of clock, cores, cache and instruction set — not any one of them.

The CPU is a silicon die inside a package; people say "CPU" for both. It runs a fixed loop: fetch an instruction, decode it, execute it, store the result. The instruction set is the fixed list of operations it can perform — add, subtract, move, compare, jump. Every program you have ever run, however complex, was broken down into those.

TermWhat it means
CoreAn independent execution unit. Four cores can run four instruction streams at once.
ThreadA stream of instructions. With SMT (Intel calls it Hyper-Threading), one core presents two threads and interleaves them — better throughput, not double the speed.
Clock speedCycles per second. Useful only between chips of the same family: a 3.4 GHz chip is not automatically faster than a 3.0 GHz chip from a newer generation.
CacheL1 (kilobytes, per core), L2 (larger), L3 (megabytes, shared). Where the CPU keeps what it is about to need again.
32-bit vs 64-bitHow wide the addresses are. A 32-bit CPU can address about 4 GB of RAM and no more, however much you install. A 64-bit CPU (and 64-bit OS) addresses vastly more.
TDPThermal design power: roughly the heat the cooler has to remove, in watts. The number that decides whether your cooler is adequate.

Sockets: LGA and PGA

LGA
Land Grid Array — the pins are on the socket (motherboard), and the CPU has flat pads. Intel's desktop sockets. Bending a socket pin is a real and expensive mistake.
PGA
Pin Grid Array — the pins are on the processor itself. AMD's AM4 and earlier. Dropping one is equally expensive, in the other direction.

What matters at build time: the socket must match the CPU exactly, and the cooler must match the socket too. The motherboard's chipset decides which CPUs it will accept, which RAM it supports, and which features exist.

Cooling

A CPU cooler pulls heat away from the die: a metal heatsink with fins for surface area, a fan or a pump to move air or liquid through it, and thermal paste in between to fill the microscopic gaps that air would otherwise leave. A pea-sized amount is enough; more is not better, and too little is worse.

Do not run a modern CPU without its cooler, not even to "check if it posts". It will post, then throttle within seconds and shut down — and a chip that shuts itself off repeatedly under thermal stress is not doing itself any favours. If you want to test without the cooler mounted, that is what the second-hand market is for.

RAM: fast, close, and forgetful

The single most useful sentence: RAM is your working set, and it is wiped on power-off. Everything else about it follows.

RAM (Random Access Memory) holds the programs and data in use right now. It is volatile: cut the power and the contents are gone. That is not a bug to be worked around, it is the trade that makes it fast enough to be useful. ROM (read-only memory) is the opposite — non-volatile, and where firmware like the BIOS lives.

TypeWhere it is usedWhy
DRAMMain memoryOne transistor and a capacitor per bit. Dense and cheap; capacitors leak, so it must be refreshed constantly. That refresh is the "dynamic".
SRAMCPU cacheSix transistors per bit. Much faster, much larger physically, far more expensive — so only megabytes of it exist.
SDRAMMain memory, olderSynchronous: it moves in step with the memory bus clock.
DDR SDRAMAll main memory since ~2000Double data rate: transfers on both the rising and falling edge of the clock, so twice per cycle. Generations DDR2 → DDR5 have each raised speed and lowered voltage.
GDDRGraphics cardsA different design tuned for massive bandwidth rather than latency. GDDR6 is not DDR6.

The physical stick, and what to check before buying

DIMM
Dual In-line Memory Module — the long desktop stick.
SODIMM
Small Outline DIMM — the short laptop stick.
Generation
DDR4 and DDR5 are not interchangeable: different notch position, different voltage, different slot. A DDR4 stick physically will not fit a DDR5 board.
Speed
Written like DDR4-3200 — 3200 megatransfers per second. Buying faster than the board supports wastes money; mixing speeds runs everything at the slowest.
Channels
Dual-channel needs two sticks in the correct paired slots (usually 2 and 4). One stick works, at roughly half the memory bandwidth.
ECC
Error-correcting memory: it detects and corrects a single-bit error instead of crashing. Requires ECC modules and a CPU and board that support it — it is not a feature of a DDR generation, which is a correction to a common handout claim.
The support question that follows from all this: "it was fine yesterday and now it crashes" on a machine with two sticks is often one bad stick. Remove one, test, swap — that is a two-minute diagnosis and a very common ticket.

The motherboard, the chipset, and the firmware

The board is the thing everything else is a guest on. Its two decisions for you are the chipset and the form factor.

The motherboard is the circuit board every component connects to: CPU socket, memory slots, expansion slots, storage connectors, power connectors, and the firmware chip that starts it all.

The chipset

The chipset manages data between the CPU, memory and peripherals. Historically it was two chips:

ChipConnected toStatus in 2026
NorthbridgeCPU ↔ RAM ↔ graphicsGone. The memory controller and graphics links moved onto the CPU die around 2009. Courses still teach it, and A+ still asks, because you may meet a machine old enough to have one.
SouthbridgeUSB, SATA, audio, network, and the rest of the slower I/OStill there, now usually just called the chipset (or the PCH).

The front-side bus (FSB) was the path between CPU and northbridge, running at a lower frequency than the CPU with a clock multiplier making up the difference — a 3200 MHz CPU on a 400 MHz bus is an 8× multiplier. Modern boards use point-to-point links instead (Intel's DMI, AMD's Infinity Fabric), so the FSB is another term you need for the exam rather than for the machine on your desk.

Form factors

ATX
The full-size standard: 305 × 244 mm, up to seven expansion slots, room for four memory sticks. The default for a powerful desktop.
Micro-ATX
Shorter (244 × 244 mm), fewer slots. Usually the sensible middle.
Mini-ITX
170 × 170 mm, one expansion slot, two memory sticks. Small builds, and you pay for the size in expansion and cooling.

The form factor has to match the case and the power supply. A Mini-ITX board fits an ATX case; an ATX board does not fit a Mini-ITX case.

Firmware: BIOS, UEFI, and the battery

BIOS is the firmware that runs when you press the button: it initialises the hardware, runs the POST (power-on self-test), finds a boot device, and hands control to the bootloader. UEFI is its replacement: faster, a graphical setup screen, support for drives over 2 TB (GPT rather than MBR), and Secure Boot, which refuses to run an unsigned bootloader.

The settings and the clock are kept alive by a small CMOS battery on the board. When it dies, the classic symptom is a machine that has forgotten the date and time and asks you to press F1 to continue.

Storage: spinning rust and flash

Two technologies, one job — and the interface between them is often the real difference.

HDDSSD

Typical80–160 MB/sSATA ~550 MB/s · NVMe 3,000–14,000 MB/s
Failure modeMechanical: clicks, bad sectors, gradual warningSilent: a controller dies and the drive is simply gone, often with no warning
£/TBCheapestMore expensive, and falling

The interface matters as much as the media

SATA
The long-standing connector. One cable for data, one for power, hot-swappable on most systems. About 550 MB/s is the ceiling — the bus, not the flash, is the limit.
NVMe
A protocol for flash over PCIe, either in an M.2 slot or a PCIe card. Thousands of MB/s and far lower latency, because it was designed for flash instead of for spinning disks.
SAS
Serial Attached SCSI: enterprise drives, SATA-shaped connectors, built for 24/7 duty and usually in servers with backplanes.
M.2
The slot, not the speed. An M.2 slot may be SATA-only, or PCIe — the same-looking slot can be three times slower. Check before buying.

Capacity, and the number that always disappoints

Drive makers sell decimal terabytes (1 TB = 1,000,000,000,000 bytes) and operating systems have historically reported binary units (1 TiB = 1,099,511,627,776 bytes). A "1 TB" drive therefore shows about 931 GB. Nobody is lying; they are using different definitions of the same word.

RAID in one paragraph

RAID 0 stripes across disks for speed with no redundancy — one disk dies, everything is gone. RAID 1 mirrors, so either disk can fail. RAID 5 stripes with parity, surviving one disk failure at the cost of one disk's capacity. RAID 10 is mirrors inside stripes: fast and resilient, expensive in disks. And the sentence that belongs on every page about RAID: RAID is not a backup — it protects against a disk dying, not against deletion, ransomware, or the enclosure being stolen.

Backups, stated as the rule they are: three copies, on two different media, with one off-site. A synced cloud folder is not a backup either — a delete syncs just as faithfully as a save.

Power: AC in, DC out

The power supply is the part people ignore until it is the answer — which is more often than you would think.

Two kinds of current. Direct current (DC) flows one way; alternating current (AC) reverses direction many times a second. The wall socket gives you AC; silicon needs DC. The PSU is the converter.

StandardWhat it is
ATOriginal. Obsolete.
ATXThe first standard with software-controlled power. Obsolete, but the name survived.
ATX12VThe common desktop standard today. Adds a dedicated CPU power connector; versions 2.x are what modular supplies quote.
EPS12VOriginally for servers, now common in high-end desktops: more 12 V current for big CPUs and GPUs.

What the numbers mean

Wattage
The ceiling on total output, volts × amps. A 650 W supply does not consume 650 W; it can deliver up to that.
Rails
Separate voltage outputs: 3.3 V and 5 V for logic and drives, 12 V for the CPU and GPU. Almost all of a modern build's power is 12 V, which is why a single strong 12 V rail matters.
Efficiency
80 PLUS ratings (White, Bronze, Silver, Gold, Platinum, Titanium) describe how much input becomes output. An inefficient supply wastes the rest as heat and noise.
Connectors
24-pin ATX for the board, 4/8-pin EPS for the CPU, 6/8-pin PCIe for the GPU, SATA power for drives, Molex for older peripherals and fans.
Voltage is pressure. Plug a 120 V device into a 220 V outlet and the excess does not politely wait — it destroys the device. That is why travel adapters that only change the plug shape are dangerous and voltage converters exist. It is also why a PSU with the wrong input switch is a one-second mistake with a permanent result.

A UPS (uninterruptible power supply) is a battery between the wall and the machine. Its job is not to keep you working through an outage; it is to ride out a brownout or give the machine two minutes to shut down cleanly, which is the difference between a power cut and a corrupted file.

Peripherals and ports

Connectors are memorised, then they are obvious. These are the ones that appear on the exam and in a ticket.

A peripheral is anything external that adds functionality: keyboard, mouse, monitor, printer, camera, external drive.

USB: a connector family, not a speed

VersionSpeedNote
USB 1.112 Mb/sHistorical
USB 2.0480 Mb/sStill everywhere, for keyboards and mice
USB 3.0 / 3.1 Gen 1 / 3.2 Gen 15 Gb/sThe same speed renamed three times — a naming mess, not a new capability
USB 3.1 Gen 2 / 3.2 Gen 210 Gb/s
USB 3.2 Gen 2×220 Gb/sNeeds a USB-C cable that is actually wired for it
USB4up to 40 Gb/sUSB-C only, shares a design with Thunderbolt 3/4

The trap: USB-C is a connector, not a speed or a feature. A USB-C port can be USB 2.0, USB 3.2, Thunderbolt, DisplayPort, power delivery, or some combination — the shape tells you nothing. Buying a cheaper cable that only carries USB 2.0 speeds is the most common version of this mistake.

Display connectors

VGA
Analogue, 15-pin, still on projectors and old kit. Image quality degrades with cable length.
DVI
Digital (and sometimes analogue too). Large connector, being retired.
HDMI
Digital video and audio on one cable. The consumer standard.
DisplayPort
Digital video and audio, designed for computers; better for high refresh rates and for daisy-chaining monitors (MST).
Thunderbolt
USB-C shape, much more capability: displays, storage and power over one cable.

Projectors, since the course names them

Three numbers decide whether a projector works in a room: lumens (brightness — you need more for a lit room than a dark one), throw ratio (how far back it must sit for a given image size), and the native resolution (anything else is scaled). Keystone correction squares up an image projected at an angle, and it costs you resolution to do it.

Mobile displays

LCD panels are backlit and block or pass light; OLED pixels emit their own light, so blacks are truly black and the panel can be thinner. OLED's trade-offs are cost, and burn-in on static content. Touch adds a digitizer layer that turns position into input — the part that fails when a screen "works but does not respond".

Building one, in the right order

The course makes you assemble a computer from scratch, because the order is the lesson: each step is made easier by the one before it.

Step zero, before anything else: avoid static. Touch a grounded metal surface, or wear an ESD strap. Static damage is usually partial and delayed — the machine works for three weeks and then does something bizarre. Components stay in their anti-static bags until the moment they are fitted.
#StepWhy here
1Open the case, check the standoffsStandoffs raise the board off the metal case; a stray one shorting the back of the board is a dead build.
2Fit the CPU to the socketDo it outside the case, where there is room. Align the triangle marker; the lever should need no force.
3Apply thermal paste, fit the coolerPaste goes between die and heatsink; the cooler is often easier to mount before the board is in.
4Fit the RAMHear the click at both ends. Check the manual for the paired slots if you have two sticks.
5Fit the M.2 drive (if any)Under a heatsink on the board, and hard to reach once everything else is in.
6Install the board into the caseNow the heavy assembly goes in.
7Fit the 3.5"/2.5" drives and cageSlide and click; SATA power and data go in next.
8Fit the PSU and connect it24-pin to the board, 8-pin to the CPU, SATA power to drives.
9Fit case fans and connect themIntake at the front, exhaust at the back and top.
10Connect the front-panel cablesPower button, reset, USB, audio. The tiny ones, and the step everyone does wrong once.
11Fit the graphics cardLast, because it blocks access to everything around it. It must click into the slot and be screwed to the case.
12Close up and power onWatch the POST, listen for beeps, check the fans spin, then get into the firmware and confirm the drive is seen.

If it does not post, the order to check is the order above: is the CPU seated, is the cooler on, is the RAM clicked in, are the two power connectors in, is the graphics card seated. Almost every "dead build" is one of those five, and the board will usually tell you which — if you have a speaker or a POST code display to hear it.

Mobile devices are the same computer, smaller

A phone has all five parts from the front of this module — plus a battery and a touchscreen, and minus your patience for screws.

PartDesktop equivalentWhat is different on mobile
SoCCPU + chipsetCPU, GPU, memory controller and radios on one chip, because space and power matter more than upgradeability.
StorageSSDSoldered flash. Not upgradeable, and the first thing to fill up.
BatteryPSU + UPSLithium-ion, measured in charge cycles. A swollen battery is a safety issue, not a performance one — stop using the device.
DisplayMonitorLCD or OLED with a digitizer bonded to it. A cracked glass usually means replacing the whole assembly.

Batteries, honestly

A lithium-ion cell degrades with every full charge cycle, which is why "battery health" exists. Heat is the other enemy: a phone left on a dashboard loses capacity faster than one kept cool. Fast charging generates heat, so it trades longevity for convenience.

Repair, and when it is not worth it

Screen
The most common repair. Glass-only repairs are possible but rare on modern bonded displays.
Battery
Common and worthwhile — if the device opens without destroying it.
Charging port
Usually a small daughterboard, often the cheapest fix that saves a phone.
Data on a dead device
The hard question. If it will not power on, data recovery may cost more than the phone is worth — which is an argument for backups, made at the worst possible moment.

In a corporate setting the question is often not "can it be fixed" but "should it be": MDM (mobile device management) lets an organisation wipe and re-provision a device remotely, which changes the economics of repair entirely. BYOD — bring your own device — is the policy question that follows: whose data is on it, and who can erase it.
