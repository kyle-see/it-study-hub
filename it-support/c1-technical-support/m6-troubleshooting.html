The essentials — hold these before the detail

The module at entry-certification level: what you must be able to do, and the facts the quizzes and drills on this page are testing. Read this first, every time.

You can — by the end of this module
  • run a fault through a repeatable sequence instead of guessing
  • ask the questions that narrow a problem in the first two minutes
  • write a ticket another technician could act on without asking you anything
  • handle an angry or non-technical user without escalating the situation
  • document a process so someone else can repeat it
Know this — the need-to-know core, 8 facts
The order
gather information, reproduce, isolate, form one hypothesis, test it, fix, verify, document. One change at a time.
Quickest first
start with the cheapest check that could eliminate the most. Cable before configuration, configuration before reinstall.
The five questions
what were you doing, what did you expect, what happened instead, when did it start, what changed?
Isolation by substitution
swap one half of the system - cable, port, account, machine. Whichever half stays broken tells you where the fault lives.
Good ticket
symptom, scope (one user or many), steps to reproduce, what was tried, current state, owner.
Documentation
error text copied verbatim with timestamps, because a paraphrase cannot be searched and the exact string can.
Soft skills
acknowledge the impact, never blame the user, say what you are doing and when you will be back, and never argue about whether the problem is real.
Escalation
escalate with evidence - what you ruled out and why - not with 'it's still broken'.
The trap: Changing three things at once. If it works afterwards you no longer know which change fixed it, and you cannot write it down for the next person.

The six-step method

The spine of every support conversation, and the answer to the interview question: how do you approach a problem you have never seen?

#StepWhat it actually means
1Identify the problemAsk questions, gather information, and reproduce it. You cannot fix what you cannot describe.
2Establish a theoryForm the most likely explanation, starting with the simplest. "The cable is loose" beats "the driver is corrupt" until proven otherwise.
3Test the theoryChange one thing. If the theory was wrong, form the next one — and note what you ruled out.
4Make a plan and do itDecide what you are going to change, what could go wrong, and how to undo it. Then change it.
5Verify and preventConfirm with the user that it is actually fixed, not just that the error stopped. Then remove the cause if you can.
6DocumentWhat it was, what you did, what you learned. The next person — possibly you in six months — starts here.
Only two of the six steps involve touching the machine. The rest are thinking, communicating and writing, which is why this module spends most of its time on questions, soft skills and documentation rather than on tools.

Ask questions, then isolate the problem

Two skills, and they are the whole of diagnosis: get information, then halve the search space.

The four questions that do the most work

What changed?
The single highest-yield question. Updates, new software, a moved cable, an expired password, a policy pushed overnight. Most faults have a cause that arrived recently.
When did it start?
An exact time turns logs into evidence. "Since this morning" and "since three weeks ago" are different investigations.
Does it happen every time?
Intermittent problems are a different class: heat, loose connections, exhausted resources, a specific sequence of actions.
Who else is affected?
One user is a machine. Everyone on the floor is a switch, a server, or a policy. Everyone everywhere is an outage.

Isolating the problem

Isolation means cutting the search space in half with each test rather than checking things in order. Does the fault follow the user to another machine, or the machine to another user? Does it happen on Wi-Fi but not Ethernet? On a different network? With a different account? Each answer eliminates half of the possibilities.

The layers from Course 2 give the same idea a structure: rule out physical, then addressing, then the service — and stop at the first layer that fails, because anything above it is unreliable until it is fixed.

Start with the quickest step first. Reboot, reseat the cable, check the power, try another port, ask whether anyone else has the problem. These are cheap, reversible, and they resolve a surprising share of tickets. The expensive diagnostic comes after the free one has failed.

Follow the cookie crumbs, and the pitfalls to avoid

One discipline that solves problems, and six habits that create them.

Follow the cookie crumbs

Treat every symptom as evidence and follow it in order. A machine that boots slowly, then drops Wi-Fi, then reports a full disk is probably one story, not three tickets: the disk filled up, the swap file could not grow, and the wireless service logged its failures into a log that could not be written. Chasing the last symptom gives you a wireless ticket. Following the trail backwards gives you the actual cause.

Jumping to a conclusion
"It is always the printer driver." The last problem you solved is not this problem. It biases every question you ask afterwards.
Tunnel vision
Twenty minutes on one theory without testing it. If two tests have not confirmed it, form another theory.
Changing two things at once
If it starts working, you do not know which change did it — and you cannot undo the wrong one.
Ignoring the user
They said "it started after the update". That was the answer, and it was in the first sentence.
Blaming the user
Even when the user caused it, saying so costs you the information you need next time. Fix the system so the mistake is harder to make.
"It works on my machine"
A statement about the difference between two machines, not a conclusion. Find the difference.
Not documenting
The fix nobody can find again is a fix you will redo. Documentation is step six for a reason.
The one that causes real damage: working outside your authority because it is faster. Changing a policy, resetting someone's password for a caller who sounds convincing, disabling antivirus to "test" — each of those is how a support technician becomes the incident.

Soft skills: the anatomy of an interaction

The eight minutes of the course that change how people experience your work, regardless of whether you fixed anything.

StageWhat good looks like
OpenGreet, give your name, and say what you are going to do. "I am going to ask a few questions and then we will get this sorted" is worth more than silence while you type.
ListenLet them finish. Take notes. Repeat the problem back in your own words — it catches misunderstandings before they cost an hour.
EmpathiseAcknowledge the impact, not the fault. "That is frustrating, and it is blocking your work" is true and useful. "You must have done something" is neither.
ExplainNo jargon. "Your computer cannot reach the file server" beats "the SMB session is failing authentication". If they ask what a word means, define it without making it a lesson.
Set expectationsSay what happens next and when. "I will call you within the hour, even if I have nothing new" is the single most trust-building sentence in support.
CloseConfirm it works from their side, ask whether anything else is wrong, tell them how to reach you, and log it.

Difficult situations

The angry user
Let them vent, do not defend, then move to facts: what exactly is happening, and what would fixed look like. Anger is usually about lost time, not about you.
You cannot fix it
Say so early, say what you have ruled out, and say what happens next. A clear "this needs the network team, and I have escalated it with these notes" is a good outcome.
You made a mistake
Say it plainly, say what you are doing about it, and do not make the user manage your embarrassment.
The user is wrong
Correct the information, never the person. "The cable is in the wrong port" and "you plugged it in wrong" describe the same fact and produce different conversations.
You do not know
"I do not know yet, and I will find out by three o'clock" is a professional answer. Inventing one is not.

Ticketing and documentation

What turns one person's fix into the team's knowledge, and the reason step six exists.

A ticket is the record of a request: who, what, when, what was tried, what fixed it. A ticketing system (ServiceNow, Jira Service Management, Freshdesk, osTicket) holds those records so they can be searched, measured and handed over.

What a ticket needs to be worth reading later

  1. The user's own words for the symptom, plus your plain-language summary of the actual problem.
  2. When it started and whether it is constant or intermittent.
  3. What changed immediately before.
  4. What you tested, and what the result was — including the tests that ruled things out, which is most of the value.
  5. What you changed, exactly. The setting, not "fixed the config".
  6. Whether it is verified fixed, and by whom.
  7. Anything worth preventing: the update that caused it, the policy that will cause it again next month.
SLA
Service Level Agreement — the promised response and resolution times, by priority. It is what "urgent" actually means in a given organisation.
Priority vs severity
Severity is how bad it is. Priority is how soon it gets done, which also depends on how many people are affected. One executive offline can outrank a hundred users on a slow report.
Escalation
Handing a ticket to someone with more authority or access, with the notes that make it actionable. Escalating without notes is just forwarding.
Knowledge base
Process documentation and known fixes, written so a new technician can follow them. The output of documentation that compounds.
Process documentation
How a repeatable job is done, step by step, including the checks and the rollback. It is what makes a task delegable.
Write for the person who takes over at 6pm. They have your ticket and nothing else — no context, no memory of the conversation, and no way to ask you. If they can act on what you wrote, you documented it well.

The interview, and the part of this module that is about you

The last third of Module 6 is job-search material, and it is not filler: it is the same skill as the rest of the module, applied to yourself.

The elevator pitch

Thirty seconds: who you are, what you can do, and what you want next. Not a life story. Here is one that works, written about nobody in particular — the shape is the lesson, the words are placeholders:

"I'm finishing an IT support certificate and a hands-on analyst program. I build and repair my own Windows and Linux machines, I'm comfortable at a command line, and I'm looking for a help desk role where I can take real tickets."

Why that shape works: every clause is something the speaker could prove, it says what the person does rather than what they are, and it ends on what they want next — which gives the interviewer somewhere to go. Write your own and keep only the clauses that would survive a follow-up question; the ones that don't are the ones that cost you the interview.

The technical interview

What they askWhat they are actually testing
"A user says their computer is slow. What do you do?"Whether you have a method, or a list of guesses. Walk the six steps out loud.
"How would you find a machine's IP address?"Whether you have done it. ipconfig, ip a, the settings screen — any of them, said with confidence.
"A user is angry and it is not your fault."Whether you will make it worse. Listen, acknowledge, move to facts.
"You do not know the answer."Whether you will pretend. "I would check the documentation, then escalate with what I found" is the right answer.
"Tell me about a problem you solved."Whether you can tell a story with a cause, an action and a result — and whether you documented it.
The move that stands out: say what you would verify, not just what you would do. Anyone can recite a fix. Saying "then I would confirm with the user that it actually works, and log what I changed" is the difference between someone who has read about support and someone who has done it.
